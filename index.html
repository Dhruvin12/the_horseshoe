Beyond the Horseshoe

This site is about Ohio Stadium, but not really the stadium itself. It's about the land around it, and how that land changed over the last 100 years.

For my first project this semester, I looked at old photos of Ohio Stadium from the Ohio State University Archives. I had one from the 1920s and one from the 1970s. The stadium looked pretty much the same in both. What surprised me was everything around it. In the 1920s photo there's a lot of empty land. By the 1970s it's full of parking lots and buildings.

So I started wondering how that happened. Who decided what went there? Was it planned? That turned into my research question:

Research question: How did Ohio State plan the development of the land around Ohio Stadium after the stadium was built, and how did the stadium influence the growth of the surrounding campus and neighborhoods?

Here's what I think, after a semester of looking into it. The land around the stadium didn't fill up by accident. Ohio State built things there because the university needed them. It needed parking for fans, dorms for more students, and space for other sports. Once the stadium was there, a lot of other buildings ended up near it.

I also think the official records only show the university's side. Students and people living nearby probably saw these changes differently, and that's something I'm still trying to find out.

I made this site for other Ohio State students, fans, and people in Columbus. If you've been to a game, you've walked through this area. You don't need to know anything about research to follow along.

Before 1919

Ohio Field gets too small

Before the Horseshoe, Ohio State played at a place called Ohio Field. It only held about 14,000 people. Football was getting more popular, and the crowds were too big for it. By 1915 an engineering professor, Thomas E. French, was pushing for a new stadium.

So the whole thing started because the university needed more seats. Ohio Field was torn down in 1924, after the new stadium opened, and other buildings went up where it used to be.

Sources: Kelly and Bona; Ohio State University Libraries, "Twelve Days of Buckeyes."

1919

92 acres by the river

In 1919, Ohio State's Board of Trustees set aside 92 acres west of Neil Avenue, next to the Olentangy River, for the stadium. The trustees are the group that makes the big decisions for the university. A lot of this land was farmland, and they actually had to wait for the crops to be harvested before they could start. The trustees didn't want to use state money, so alumni raised $1 million in donations instead.

To me this is the most important decision in the whole story. The stadium didn't need all 92 acres, so the university had extra land right next to it. The city and the neighbors didn't make this call. The trustees did. I want to read the trustee records from this time to see if they were already planning what to do with the rest of the land.

Sources: Kelly and Bona; Lopresti; Ohio State University Libraries, "Twelve Days of Buckeyes."

1921–1922

Building it

The architect was Howard Dwight Smith, who went to Ohio State. He designed the horseshoe shape with two levels of seats. The land was low and right by the river, so it could flood. To make it work, they straightened a bend in the river and moved the drainage ditches into sewers. They even built a small railroad just to bring in sand, gravel and wood.

The first game was against Ohio Wesleyan on October 7, 1922. The official dedication was at the Michigan game two weeks later, on October 21.

I didn't know before this project that they changed the river. It shows that even at the very start, the university was willing to change the land to fit the stadium.

Sources: Lopresti; Ohio State University Libraries, "Twelve Days of Buckeyes."

1920s–1940s

Mostly open land

For a long time after the stadium opened, there wasn't much around it. University records mention polo fields south of the stadium. Those fields flooded when the river got high in 1927 and again in 1937.

One thing I found interesting is that the stadium was used for more than football. For a while, students who didn't have much money lived inside it as a cheap dorm. I'd like to find out more about that, like who lived there and when it stopped.

This is what my 1920s photo shows. A big stadium with a lot of space around it.

Source: Ohio State University Libraries, "Twelve Days of Buckeyes."

1944–1956

The arena that almost got attached

In 1944, there was a plan to attach an eight-sided building to the north end of the stadium. It would have been a field house for other sports. People were against it, and the plan got dropped. Instead, St. John Arena was built about 500 feet away. It was finished in November 1956, and French Field House was built right next to it. Smith designed the arena too.

This fits what I think was going on. When the university needed a basketball arena, it put it right by the stadium. But this is also a time when people pushed back and actually changed the plan. I want to look in old issues of The Lantern from 1944 to find out who was against it.

Sources: Kelly and Bona; Ohio State University Libraries, "St. John Arena & French Field House."

1965–1967

The Towers

Lincoln and Morrill Towers were started in 1965 and finished in August 1967. They're the two tall dorms on Cannon Drive, right next to the stadium. Each one is 26 stories and held 1,920 students. They were also the first dorms on campus where men and women both lived.

The original plan was actually six towers, not two. Only two got built. According to the university archives, people on campus argued a lot about where to put them, and the records don't say for sure why the other four never happened.

Ohio State was growing fast in the 1960s and needed housing. It already owned the open land near the stadium, so that's where the towers went. This is when the area starts to look like my 1970s photo.

Sources: DePaola; Ohio State University Libraries, "Cramped Quarters at the Towers" and "Lincoln Tower."

1970s

Filled in

By my 1970s photo, most of the open land is gone. There are parking lots, the arena, the field house and the towers all around the stadium. The stadium itself still looks almost the same as it did 50 years earlier.

I think this makes sense if you think about what a stadium needs. You can't have tens of thousands of people show up for a game with nowhere to park. And a university that keeps getting bigger needs somewhere to put students and teams. Because the stadium was already there, a lot of that ended up next to it.

Source: my Major Project 1 archive photos.

1990s–2010s

Moving east

The stadium got updated and made bigger a few times, including a big renovation that was done in 2001. Around this same time, Ohio State started working on the neighborhoods east of campus. It set up a group called Campus Partners, put out A Plan for High Street in 2000, built the South Campus Gateway, and later got involved in rebuilding Weinland Park.

This is what my main source from MP2, Sayoni Bose, writes about. She found that when Ohio State moved into those neighborhoods, it listened to homeowners and businesses a lot more than poor renters. The stadium side was different because the university already owned most of it. There weren't as many neighbors there to push back.

Sources: Bose; Campus Partners; Ohio State University Libraries, "Twelve Days of Buckeyes."

Today

Now

Today the stadium is surrounded by St. John Arena, French Field House, the Towers, and a lot of parking. On game days the whole area is basically there for the stadium. The rest of the week, students use a lot of it.

Looking at all of this together, I keep coming back to the same thing. The university made almost every decision about this land, and it usually made them based on what it needed at the time. What I don't know yet is what students and neighbors thought about it back then. That's the next thing I'm working on.

Why it changed

The timeline shows what changed. This page is about why. Most of it comes from my main source from MP2.

The article is "Universities and the Redevelopment Politics of the Neoliberal City" by Sayoni Bose, from 2015. Bose went to grad school at Ohio State, and she used Ohio State as her example. Her main point is that universities today act a lot like businesses. They compete with other schools for students, money and rankings, and to keep up they have to grow. But a university can't just pack up its buildings and move somewhere else. So it grows where it already is, which makes the land next to campus really valuable to it.

She uses some words that were hard for me at first. Click on each one to see what it means. I put them in order because each one kind of leads to the next.

Neoliberalism

Basically the idea that everything should run like a business. Bose says this way of thinking has been shaping American cities since the 1970s. For a university, it means always feeling pressure to grow.

↓
Entrepreneurial subject

Bose got this term from a writer named Michel Foucault. It means a school that thinks like a company and takes charge of rebuilding the area around it. This was the part that helped me understand why Ohio State does what it does, and not just what it does.

↓
Redevelopment

Rebuilding an area, usually by tearing down what was there before. Bose points out that it sounds like a harmless word, but somebody usually loses something.

↓
Subjectivities

How people see themselves and other people. Bose says Ohio State used existing splits between rich and poor, and between races, to get homeowners and businesses on its side. Some poor renters ended up being treated like they were the problem.

↓
Gentrification

When people with more money move into a poorer area and the people who already lived there get pushed out. Bose says Ohio State brought new stores to High Street as a first step toward changing Weinland Park.

What this has to do with the stadium

Bose wrote about the east side of campus, mainly High Street and Weinland Park. She doesn't really talk about the stadium. But her question works for my project too: why does a university care so much about the land right next to it?

I think the stadium acted like an anchor. Once there was a giant stadium on the river that wasn't going anywhere, other big projects got built near it. The university was already there and already owned the land. If you look at a map, the arena, the field house, the towers and the parking lots are all bunched up around the Horseshoe.

There's one big difference from what Bose studied, though. On the east side, Ohio State had to deal with homeowners, renters and business owners. Around the stadium, the university had owned most of the land since 1919. That might be why there wasn't as much fighting over it. It also probably means the people most affected were students, and it's harder to find what students thought in official records.

Other sources I'm checking

Bose's reference list led me to two more sources. One is a book by David Perry and Wim Wiewel called The University as Urban Developer, which has examples of universities building in their cities. The other is an article by Ray Bromley and Robert Kent called "Integrating beyond the Campus," about Ohio's city universities, including Ohio State, and their work in nearby neighborhoods. Bose says these kinds of studies explain what universities do but not why. I'm reading them to see if what happened around the stadium matches what Bose describes.
